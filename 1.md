## 1. Definitions

**Sound**\
A mechanical disturbance that propagates through a medium (air, water, or solid). When it reaches an auditory system, it may produce an auditory sensation.

**Audio Signal**\
An analogue or digital representation of sound.

**Analogue Signal**\
Continuous in time and amplitude; closely follows the original sound.

**Digital Signal**\
Sampled in time (discrete values) and stored and processed in digital form.

**Amplitude (A)**\
The magnitude of a wave. It contributes to perceived loudness.

**Period (T)**\
The time required for one complete cycle. Measured in seconds (s).

**Wavelength (λ)**\
The physical length of one cycle. Measured in metres (m).

**Frequency (f)**\
The number of cycles per second. Measured in hertz (Hz). Strongly related to perceived pitch.

**Waveform**\
The shape of a wave. Influences harmonic content and timbre.

**Loudness**\
The perceptual attribute by which sounds are judged from quiet to loud.

**Decibel (dB)**\
A logarithmic way of expressing relative level or change between sounds or signals.

**Timbre**\
Distinguishes sounds by their "colour", influenced by harmonic content and waveform shape. It allows listeners to distinguish instruments and voices even when they have the same pitch and loudness.

**Pitch**\
The perceptual attribute by which sounds can be ordered from low to high.

**Note Names**\
A system using the first seven letters of the alphabet, with sharps (#) and flats (b). The chromatic scale is:

A, A#, B, C, C#, D, D#, E, F, F#, G, G#.

**Octave**\
The interval between two frequencies where one is twice the other (ratio 2:1). Perceived as the same pitch class.

**Semitone**\
The interval between two adjacent musical notes. One octave contains 12 semitones.

**Cent**\
One hundredth of a semitone. One semitone = 100 cents. One octave = 1200 cents.

**Music (for this module)**\
Intentionally organised sound and silence used for musical expression or interaction.

**Rhythm**\
The organisation of sound and silence in time.

**Melody**\
A sequence of pitches that creates a recognisable tune.

**Harmony**\
The combination of multiple notes played at the same time.

**Acoustics**\
Asks: What is happening physically? (Physical sound.)

**Psychoacoustics**\
Asks: How is sound perceived? (Perceived sound.)

**Noise**\
Has three meanings:

1. **Everyday listening:** Unwanted sound.
2. **Signal processing:** An unwanted disturbance in a signal.
3. **Synthesis:** A deliberately generated stochastic signal.

**Musical Sound**\
Has a more regular or periodic structure and is often perceived as having a definite pitch.

## 2. Explanations

### 2.1 Sound-to-Audio Signal Chain

1. A mechanical disturbance or vibrating source produces sound.
2. Sound propagates through a medium (air, water, or solid) as a mechanical wave consisting of pressure variations.
3. The wave reaches the auditory system, such as the ear.
4. It may produce an auditory sensation.

### 2.2 Properties of a Sound Wave

The properties of a sound wave can be represented as parameters that can be calculated, generated, and modified in software.

| Property       | What it is                                       | Perceptual link             |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------- |
| Amplitude (A)  | Magnitude of the wave                            | Loudness                    |
| Period (T)     | Time required for one cycle, measured in seconds | —                           |
| Wavelength (λ) | Physical length of one cycle, measured in metres | —                           |
| Frequency (f)  | Number of cycles per second, measured in hertz   | Pitch                       |
| Waveform       | Shape of the wave                                | Timbre and harmonic content |

**Frequency examples:**

- 1 Hz = 1 complete cycle in 1 second.
- 2 Hz = 2 complete cycles in 1 second.
- 4 Hz = 4 complete cycles in 1 second.

Higher frequency → more cycles per second → higher pitch.

Lower frequency → fewer cycles per second → lower pitch.

### 2.3 Amplitude

- More energy transferred into the medium generally produces a larger amplitude and a louder sound.
- The relationship between amplitude and perceived loudness is not entirely straightforward.
- Changing amplitude while keeping frequency constant changes the signal's magnitude.

| Scaling factor | Effect         |
| -------------- | -------------- |
| `0`            | Silence        |
| `0 < k < 1`    | Quieter signal |
| `1`            | No change      |
| `k > 1`        | Louder signal  |

In programming, changing amplitude usually means multiplying the signal by a scaling factor.

### 2.4 Loudness

- Greater amplitude generally produces greater perceived loudness.
- Loudness also depends on:
  - **Frequency:** Human hearing is more sensitive to some frequencies than others.
  - **Duration:** Longer sounds can be perceived as louder than short sounds with the same amplitude.
  - **Hearing sensitivity:** Individual hearing characteristics affect perception.
- The outer ear collects sound, the inner ear converts vibrations into signals, and the brain processes these signals to perceive loudness.

### 2.5 Decibels (dB)

**Why use decibels?**

- They make very large ranges of signal levels easier to describe.
- They are useful for comparing changes in signal level.
- They provide a logarithmic way to describe gain and attenuation.

**Common interpretations:**

- `0 dB` change: No change relative to the reference level.
- Positive dB change: Increase in level.
- Negative dB change: Decrease in level.

A decibel value depends on the reference being used. A value of `0 dB` does not necessarily mean silence.

**Useful reference ideas:**

- Negative dB values indicate a lower level relative to the reference.
- Positive dB values indicate a higher level relative to the reference.
- A change of approximately **6 dB** corresponds to roughly doubling or halving the amplitude ratio.

**Amplitude scaling examples:**

| Scaling factor | Effect           |
| -------------- | ---------------- |
| `0`            | Silence          |
| `0.5`          | Lower amplitude  |
| `1`            | No change        |
| `2`            | Higher amplitude |

In programming, a signal can be scaled to make it quieter or louder, and the resulting level change can be expressed in decibels.

### 2.6 Timbre

**Definition:** Timbre distinguishes sounds by their tone colour or quality. It is influenced by harmonic content and waveform shape.

Two sounds can have the same pitch and similar loudness but still sound different because of their timbre. For example, a violin, flute, and human voice can produce the same note, such as A4 at 440 Hz, but sound different.

**Factors that shape timbre:**

- **Harmonic content:** The relative strengths of harmonics affect brightness, richness, and tone.
- **Waveform shape:** Different waveform shapes, resulting from different harmonic content, produce different timbres.
- **Temporal envelope (optional):** Describes how a sound changes over time through attack, decay, sustain, and release.

**Emotional impact:**

- **Bright timbre:** Often associated with excitement or energy. It generally contains more prominent high-frequency components and may sound sharper or more lively.
- **Mellow timbre:** Often associated with calmness or warmth. It may contain less prominent high-frequency components and sound smoother or rounder.

**Importance in music programming:**

- Creates unique digital instruments.
- Shapes the character of music production and sound design.
- Helps simulate acoustic instruments realistically.

### 2.7 Pitch

- Pitch is the perceptual attribute by which sounds are ordered from low to high.
- Fundamental frequency strongly influences pitch, although spectral content and other signal properties may also affect perception.
- **Logarithmic perception:** Humans perceive pitch primarily in terms of frequency ratios rather than equal frequency differences.

For example, the perceived interval between 100 Hz and 200 Hz is an octave, as is the interval between 1000 Hz and 2000 Hz.

**Simple and complex tones:**

- **Sound A:** A sine wave containing only the fundamental frequency.
- **Sound B:** A complex tone containing a fundamental frequency and harmonics.

The additional harmonics in a complex tone influence its timbre.

### 2.8 Note Names and Octaves

Pitches that double in frequency have the same note name in different octaves.

- 440 Hz = A4.
- 880 Hz = A5.

An octave is defined by a **frequency ratio**, not by a fixed frequency difference.

**Examples:**

- 100 Hz → 200 Hz = one octave (ratio 2:1).
- 1000 Hz → 1100 Hz = not one octave.
- 1000 Hz → 2000 Hz = one octave (ratio 2:1).

Equal octave steps on a logarithmic scale:

100 Hz → 200 Hz → 400 Hz → 800 Hz.

Each step represents one octave.

**Key takeaway:** Equal frequency differences do not produce equal pitch intervals. Equal frequency ratios produce equal octave intervals.

### 2.9 Cents, Semitones, and Octaves

- **Semitone:** The interval between two adjacent musical notes, such as A to A#.
- **One octave:** 12 semitones in twelve-tone equal temperament.
- **One semitone:** 100 cents.
- **One octave:** 1200 cents.

These units are useful for describing musical intervals and small changes in pitch.

### 2.10 Musical Elements

Music usually combines rhythm, melody, harmony, and timbre to create emotionally significant compositions.

| Element | Definition                                                             | Visual cue                   |
| ------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Rhythm  | Organisation of sound and silence in time                              | Note durations on a timeline |
| Melody  | Sequence of pitches creating a recognisable tune                       | Notes on a staff             |
| Harmony | Multiple notes played at the same time                                 | Stacked notes or chords      |
| Timbre  | Unique tone colour or quality, such as differences between instruments | Different waveform shapes    |

### 2.11 Acoustics vs Psychoacoustics

**Acoustics** focuses on physical sound:

- Sound sources, such as loudspeakers.
- The medium through which sound travels, such as air or water.
- Measurable properties, such as sound pressure.
- Physical wave properties, including amplitude and frequency.

**Psychoacoustics** focuses on perceived sound:

- The listener's hearing, context, and experience.
- Perceived pitch.
- Perceived loudness.
- Perceived timbre.

**Key takeaway:** The same physical sound can be perceived differently depending on hearing, context, and experience.

### 2.12 Noise vs Musical Sound

Distinguishing noise from musical sounds is useful for filtering, synthesis, and audio analysis in music programming.

| Musical sound                                      | Noise                                                            |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Generally has a more regular or periodic structure | Often has an irregular or random structure                       |
| Often perceived as having a definite pitch         | Often lacks a definite pitch                                     |
| Used in synthesis and audio analysis               | Used creatively in synthesis, sound design, and audio processing |

Noise is used creatively to generate:

- Drum sounds.
- Wind effects.
- Textures.
- Risers.
- Special effects.

## 3. Code

### 3.1 Building a Time Array

```
sample_rate = 2000
duration = 0.04

time = np.arange(
    int(sample_rate * duration)
) / sample_rate
```

**Workflow:**

1. **Sample rate and duration:** `2000 Hz` and `0.04 s`.
2. **Number of samples:** `2000 × 0.04 = 80`.
3. **Sample indices:** `np.arange(80)` produces indices from `0` to `79`.
4. **Time array:** Dividing the indices by `2000` converts them into time values in seconds.

**Explanation:**

- `sample_rate = 2000` means 2000 samples per second.
- `duration = 0.04` means the signal lasts 0.04 seconds, or 40 milliseconds.
- `int(sample_rate * duration)` calculates the number of samples: `2000 × 0.04 = 80`.
- `np.arange(80)` generates sample indices from `0` to `79`.
- Dividing by `sample_rate` converts the sample indices into time values in seconds.

**Result:**

| Property          | Value    |
| ----------------- | -------- |
| Number of samples | 80       |
| Time step (Δt)    | 0.0005 s |
| Array shape       | `(80,)`  |
| Start time        | 0.0 s    |
| End time          | 0.0395 s |

The result is an array containing 80 evenly spaced time values, with a time step of 0.0005 seconds.

This time array is later used to generate waveform samples.

### 3.2 Generating Waveforms (`WaveProperties.py`)

```
def generate_wave(
    time,
    frequency,
    amplitude=1.0,
    shape="sine"
):
    phase = 2 * np.pi * frequency * time

    if shape == "sine":
        wave = np.sin(phase)
    elif shape == "square":
        wave = signal.square(phase)
    elif shape == "triangle":
        wave = signal.sawtooth(
            phase,
            width=0.5
        )
    elif shape == "sawtooth":
        wave = signal.sawtooth(phase)
    else:
        raise ValueError("Unsupported waveform shape.")

    return amplitude * wave
```

**Overview:**

`generate_wave()` creates a periodic waveform from a sequence of time values. The user chooses the frequency, amplitude, and waveform shape. The function returns a NumPy array containing the generated samples.

**Parameters:**

| Parameter       | Meaning                                                                                                                   |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `time`          | NumPy array of sample times in seconds. Its length determines how many samples are generated.                             |
| `frequency`     | Number of waveform cycles per second, measured in hertz. Higher frequency produces more cycles in the same time interval. |
| `amplitude=1.0` | Peak magnitude. Increasing amplitude makes the waveform taller without changing its frequency.                            |
| `shape="sine"`  | Waveform type: `"sine"`, `"square"`, `"triangle"`, or `"sawtooth"`.                                                       |

**Phase calculation:**

The function calculates the phase angle for every time sample using:

```
phase = 2 * np.pi * frequency * time
```

Mathematically:

[\
\phi(t) = 2\pi ft\
]

where:

- (\phi(t)) is the phase angle in radians.
- (f) is the frequency in hertz.
- (t) is time in seconds.

**Waveform selection:**

| Shape string | Implementation                      | Result                  |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| `"sine"`     | `np.sin(phase)`                     | Sine wave               |
| `"square"`   | `signal.square(phase)`              | Square wave             |
| `"triangle"` | `signal.sawtooth(phase, width=0.5)` | Symmetric triangle wave |
| `"sawtooth"` | `signal.sawtooth(phase)`            | Rising sawtooth wave    |

If the waveform name is unsupported, the function raises a `ValueError`.

**Returned result:**

The selected waveform is multiplied by the specified amplitude and returned as a NumPy array of samples.

```
return amplitude * wave
```

**Important:** The function requires NumPy and SciPy's signal module to be imported. For example:

```
import numpy as np
from scipy import signal
```

### 3.3 Amplitude Scaling (Programming Connection)

```
quieter = signal * 0.5
louder = signal * 2.0
```

Multiplying a signal by a scaling factor changes its amplitude:

- `signal * 0.5` halves the amplitude.
- `signal * 2.0` doubles the amplitude.

In music programming, a signal can be scaled to make it quieter or louder, and the resulting level change can be expressed in decibels.

The change in sound level in decibels is calculated using:

**Change in decibels = 20 × log10(new amplitude / original amplitude)**

- Doubling the amplitude increases the level by approximately 6 dB.
- Halving the amplitude decreases the level by approximately 6 dB.
- Keeping the amplitude unchanged produces a change of 0 dB.

For example:

- Halving the amplitude gives approximately `−6 dB`.
- Doubling the amplitude gives approximately `+6 dB`.

These values describe the change relative to the original signal.
